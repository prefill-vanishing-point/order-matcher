# Order Matcher

Turns free-text restaurant orders like *"5 lb bananas, cs kale, 2 cases crimini"* into catalog SKUs, using [Jev](https://docs.typesafe.ai/api)'s Choice questions, and flags the lines a person should check.

The design follows Specialty Produce's public write-up of how they match restaurant orders with Jev. This project rebuilds that design on synthetic data so I could work through the product decisions behind it. It is not affiliated with Specialty Produce or TypeSafe.

**Result:** on 100 held-out orders, Jev matched 96.2% of lines exactly. The 80% of lines confident enough to skip review were all correct, and every wrong line was sent to review.

## The problem

A produce distributor gets orders by text, voicemail and fax, written however the chef likes: "cs kale", "5# bananas", "2 cases baby bellas", "grass x2". Every line has to become exactly one SKU and unit from the catalog.

- **Done by hand**, this is slow work for the order desk.
- **Done wrong**, the restaurant gets the wrong product and the distributor issues a credit.

The goal is to match most lines automatically and to know which matches not to trust.

**Who it's for**
- **Order desk staff:** instead of keying every line, they check only the flagged ones.
- **Chefs:** nothing changes for them. They keep ordering the way they always have.

## What success looks like

| Metric | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Exact item + unit | The real outcome: the right thing on the truck |
| Right product | Separates "wrong product" from "right product, wrong unit" |
| Accuracy of lines that skip review | If staff can't trust these, they re-check everything and the tool saves no time |
| Share of lines sent to review | The work left for staff |
| Wrong lines caught by review | Mistakes that aren't caught reach the customer |
| Time per order | Specialty Produce's previous LLM pipeline took 6 to 39 seconds per order |

The main dial is the review threshold. Raising it lets fewer errors through but gives staff more lines to check.

## How it works

```
order text ─▶ split into lines ─▶ for every line, in parallel:

  1. Jev chooses from what this customer ordered in the last 90 days, or "none of these"
     (if the chef named a unit, each product is offered once, in that unit)
  2. "none of these", or confidence < 0.85?
        keyword-search what all customers ordered recently, ask Jev again
  3. chef named a unit (lb / case / each)?  switch to that unit of the chosen product
  4. confidence ≥ 0.90 → accept      otherwise → review
```

**Why these choices**

- **The customer's own recent purchases are the options.** A Jev Choice takes at most 255 options, and a real catalog has thousands of SKU and unit combinations. Most lines are reorders, so the customer's last 90 days is a short list that usually contains the answer. Each option also says how often the customer ordered it, which helps Jev pick the usual unit when the chef leaves it out.
- **"None of these" is always an option.** Without it, Jev has to pick something even when the right product isn't listed, and it can be confidently wrong. See [What I learned](#what-i-learned).
- **A second pass only when needed.** It handles products the customer hasn't ordered recently. The second list combines the first pass's top five guesses with products any customer ordered recently that share a word with the line.
- **Jev picks the product, a plain rule picks the unit.** If the chef wrote "5 lb", the line should be per pound, whatever the model chose. The rule is cheap, predictable and easy to explain to staff.
- **One request per line, all in parallel.** An order takes as long as its slowest line, not the sum of all its lines.
- **Thresholds of 0.85 and 0.90 come from the original write-up.** They should be re-tuned on real orders.

## Data

All data is synthetic, generated by `scripts/generate_data.py` from a fixed seed:

- 158 SKUs (about 80 products in case, per-lb or each units)
- 40 restaurants with 150 days of history (17,591 purchases)
- A **dev set** of 30 orders (259 lines), used for every change described below
- A **test set** of 100 orders (716 lines), run once at the end for the results

Order lines mix abbreviations (`cs`, `#`, `bu`), typos, chef slang ("grass" for asparagus, "razz", "pasillas"), all caps and missing units. About 15% of lines are products the customer hasn't ordered in the last 90 days. Every line is labelled with the correct SKU.

**Limits of synthetic data:** real orders are messier, real catalogs have thousands of SKUs and size variants, and real customers have habits. Transcribing voicemail and splitting an order into lines are outside the scope of this project.

## Results

Test set, 100 orders and 716 lines. For reference, the second column is a local stand-in that answers in Jev's format using string similarity only, so the project runs without an API key.

| Metric | **Jev** | Stand-in |
|---|---|---|
| Exact item + unit | **96.2%** | 92.6% |
| Right product | **96.4%** | 92.6% |
| Lines that skip review (confidence ≥ 0.90) | **79.7%** | 74.7% |
| …of which are right | **100%** | 99.1% |
| Lines sent to review | **20.3%** | 25.3% |
| Wrong lines caught by review | **100%** | 90.6% |
| Time per order, median / 95th percentile | 1.2 s / 2.1 s | 0.04 s / 0.14 s |

**What this shows:**
- **No wrong line skipped review.** Staff can accept the 80% that pass without re-checking them, which is the point of the tool.
- **Most of Jev's 27 misses come from slang and typos, and the search step causes more of them than Jev does.** In 20 of them, Jev answered "none of these":
  - **13 are search misses.** The customer hadn't ordered the product recently, and the keyword search couldn't connect the word to it ("cukes", "razz", "spearmint"). Jev never saw the right option.
  - **7 are Jev misses.** The right product was in the list, but Jev didn't recognise the word ("rocket" for arugula, "grass" for asparagus, the typo "cuali").
- **One in five lines still needs a person.** Cutting that without letting errors through is the next problem to work on.

## What I learned

Every change below was made and measured on the dev set. The test set was run only once, at the end.

1. **Instruction wording matters.** My first instruction to Jev ended "Ignore the quantity". Jev treated "5 pounds" as part of the quantity and split "5 pounds bananas" between bananas by the pound (0.72) and by the case (0.27). Spelling out which words are units helped, but didn't fix it.
2. **Let the model pick the product and a rule pick the unit.** When the chef names a unit, each product is now offered only once, in that unit. Jev no longer has to choose between two units of the same product, and "5 pounds bananas" went from 0.53 confidence (sent to review) to 1.00.
3. **A Choice can't say "none of these" unless you offer it.** On the dev set, about a third of Jev's mistakes skipped review at 0.96–0.97 confidence. "Granny smith" became Fuji apples, "chives" became scallions and "russets" became fingerling potatoes. In each case the customer had never ordered the right product, so it wasn't among the options, and Jev confidently picked the nearest one. The original design only searches more widely when confidence is low, so it has the same blind spot. Adding a "none of these" option changed the dev results like this:

   | Dev set | Before | After |
   |---|---|---|
   | Exact item + unit | 94.6% | 97.3% |
   | Wrong lines caught by review | 64% | 100% |
   | Lines sent to review | 13% | 23% |

   I'd make that trade: more lines to check, but none of the mistakes reach a customer.

## Running it

Python 3.10 or later, standard library only, nothing to install.

```powershell
python -m order_matcher customers                     # restaurants and what they order
python -m order_matcher match C003 "5 pounds bananas, a case of kale, 2 cases of crimini, cs grass"
python -m order_matcher evaluate --show-errors 10     # accuracy check on the test set
python -m order_matcher evaluate --set dev
python -m unittest                                    # tests
python scripts/generate_data.py                       # rebuild data/ (same seed, same files)
```

**With Jev:** save your key as a user environment variable named `TYPESAFE_API_KEY`. On Windows: Start → "Edit environment variables for your account" → New. Then open a new terminal. Once the key is set, the commands use Jev automatically. You can choose explicitly with `--decider jev` or `--decider local`.

```powershell
python -m order_matcher evaluate --decider jev
python -m order_matcher match C003 "5 lb bananas" --show-request   # print the request Jev receives
```

The request format follows [docs.typesafe.ai/api](https://docs.typesafe.ai/api) as of October 2026. Jev is in early access, so the format may change.

## Web app

A page where anyone can paste an order, watch Jev match it, and work through the flagged lines the way order desk staff would: keep the suggestion, pick a next-best guess, or choose any catalog item. The finished order copies out as CSV.

**Run it on your computer.** It needs one package, the [waitress](https://docs.pylonsproject.org/projects/waitress/) web server:

```powershell
python -m venv .venv
.venv\Scripts\python.exe -m pip install -r requirements.txt
.venv\Scripts\python.exe -m order_matcher serve      # then open http://127.0.0.1:8000
```

**Put it online with Render** (free tier), so you can send people a link:

1. Push this repo to GitHub.
2. In [Render](https://render.com), choose **New → Blueprint** and connect the repo. Render reads `render.yaml`.
3. When Render asks for `TYPESAFE_API_KEY`, paste your Jev key. It is stored in Render, not in the repo.
4. Deploy. Render gives you an address like `https://order-matcher-xxxx.onrender.com`.

Free Render services go to sleep after about 15 minutes without visitors, and the first visit afterwards takes up to a minute while the service wakes up. Before a meeting, open the link a minute early.

**How the key and your credit are protected**

- The browser never sees the key. It talks only to this server, and the server calls Jev.
- Each order is capped at 25 lines and 2,000 characters.
- Each visitor can match 20 orders per 10 minutes, and the whole site 300 orders a day. Change these with the `ORDERS_PER_VISITOR` and `ORDERS_PER_DAY` environment variables.
- The same order for the same restaurant is answered from memory instead of calling Jev again, so clicking the examples costs nothing after the first time.
- Pages are served with a strict Content Security Policy and can't be embedded in other sites.

| Address | What it does |
|---|---|
| `GET /` | The page |
| `GET /api/bootstrap` | Restaurants, catalog and examples for the page |
| `POST /api/match` | Matches one order: `{"customer_id": "C003", "order": "2 cs kale, 5# limes"}` |
| `GET /healthz` | Returns `ok`, for the host's health check |

## Next steps

1. **Teach the system chef vocabulary**, which is behind most of the remaining misses. Learn aliases from staff corrections ("cukes" → cucumbers) and use them in two places: in the keyword search, and in the option descriptions Jev reads, such as "Arugula, baby (rocket)". The search could also match parts of words ("mint" in "spearmint"). Measure on a fresh test set, because this one has now been seen.
2. **Shadow mode.** Run on real orders next to the order desk without acting on the output, then compare.
3. **Tune thresholds on real data.** Plot review rate against accuracy for each threshold, possibly per customer.
4. **Connect the review screen to a real order system.** The web app's review step ends in a CSV today. Confirmed lines could also feed back as purchase history.
5. **Speed.** The median is 1.2 s per order, against the 0.41 s reported in the original write-up. The second pass adds a full round trip for about a third of lines.

```
  deciders.py   Jev client and the local stand-in

```
